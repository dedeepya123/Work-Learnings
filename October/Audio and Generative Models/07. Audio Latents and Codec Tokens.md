# 06 - Audio Latents and Codec Tokens: What Exactly Is the Model Generating?

## Recap

In the previous lesson, we learned that modern audio systems often use neural codecs such as:

```text
SoundStream
EnCodec
```

instead of relying entirely on mel spectrograms.

Conceptually:

```text
Audio
 ↓
Codec Encoder
 ↓
Learned Representation
 ↓
Codec Decoder
 ↓
Audio
```

This learned representation is what makes modern audio generation practical.

However, this raises an important question:

> What exactly is this learned representation?

When people say things like:

```text
Codec Tokens
Audio Latents
Audio Features
Embeddings
```

what do they actually mean?

Let's build an intuition.

---

## Think About Language Models First

Suppose we have a sentence:

```text
"I love AI"
```

The pipeline looks something like:

```text
Text
 ↓
Tokens
 ↓
Embeddings
 ↓
Transformer
 ↓
Hidden States
 ↓
Output Tokens
```

Notice that different representations exist at different stages.

We have:

```text
Tokens
```

which are discrete identifiers.

We have:

```text
Embeddings
```

which are dense vectors.

We have:

```text
Hidden States
```

which are deeper learned representations.

The same idea appears in audio.

---

## Audio Has Multiple Levels of Representation Too

Consider:

```text
Audio
 ↓
Codec Encoder
 ↓
Learned Representation
 ↓
Codec Decoder
 ↓
Audio
```

The learned representation sitting in the middle can take different forms.

Sometimes it is:

```text
Continuous Vectors
```

Sometimes it is:

```text
Discrete Tokens
```

Both approaches are common.

---

# Part 1: Audio Latents

Let's start with audio latents.

---

## What Is a Latent?

A latent is simply:

> A compressed learned representation that captures important information about the input.

For example:

```text
Audio
 ↓
Encoder
 ↓
Latent
```

Instead of storing:

```text
240,000 waveform samples
```

we might represent the same speech using:

```text
A few hundred vectors
```

Those vectors are called latents.

---

## Why Do Latents Exist?

Suppose we record:

```text
"Hello"
```

The waveform may contain tens of thousands of values.

Yet much of that information is redundant.

The encoder learns:

```text
What information is important
```

and

```text
What information can be compressed
```

The result becomes a smaller representation:

```text
Audio
 ↓
Encoder
 ↓
Latent
```

This latent is not human-readable.

You cannot listen to it.

You cannot directly interpret it.

But the decoder understands it.

---

## An Analogy with Images

Imagine image generation.

Many modern image systems do:

```text
Image
 ↓
Encoder
 ↓
Latents
 ↓
Decoder
 ↓
Image
```

The latent might look like:

```python
[64, 64, 4]
```

instead of:

```python
[512, 512, 3]
```

The same idea applies in audio.

```text
Audio
 ↓
Latents
 ↓
Audio
```

The model operates in a compressed space.

---

## Mental Model

Think of audio latents as:

```text
Compressed Audio Knowledge
```

They are not sound.

They are not words.

They are not mel spectrograms.

They are learned vectors that contain enough information to reconstruct the original audio.

---

# Part 2: Codec Tokens

Now let's look at another idea.

Instead of continuous vectors, we can represent audio using discrete symbols.

---

## A Reminder About Tokens

In NLP:

```text
"I love AI"
```

becomes:

```text
[15, 483, 901]
```

These numbers are discrete identifiers.

We call them tokens.

---

## Applying This Idea to Audio

A codec can learn a vocabulary of audio patterns.

Instead of storing audio as continuous vectors:

```text
[0.15, -0.22, 0.91, ...]
```

the encoder may output:

```text
[25, 81, 42, 12, 77]
```

These are codec tokens.

Conceptually:

```text
Audio
 ↓
Codec Encoder
 ↓
Codec Tokens
 ↓
Codec Decoder
 ↓
Audio
```

---

## Why Is This Useful?

Think about what LLMs generate.

They generate:

```text
Tokens
```

not raw text characters.

Similarly, an audio model can generate:

```text
Codec Tokens
```

instead of generating waveforms.

This makes generation significantly easier.

---

## The Audio Vocabulary Analogy

Think of codec tokens as:

```text
Audio Vocabulary
```

Just as language models learn relationships between:

```text
Words
Sentences
Grammar
```

Audio models can learn relationships between:

```text
Speech Sounds
Voice Patterns
Acoustic Structures
```

represented as codec tokens.

---

# Continuous vs Discrete Representations

At this point we have encountered two possibilities.

---

## Continuous Latents

```text
Audio
 ↓
Latent Vectors
```

Example:

```python
[B, T, D]
```

such as:

```python
[B, 100, 96]
```

which looks very similar to transformer hidden states.

---

## Codec Tokens

```text
Audio
 ↓
Discrete Tokens
```

Example:

```python
[B, T]
```

containing token IDs.

---

## Comparing With NLP

A useful analogy is:

```text
Text Tokens
```

are similar to:

```text
Codec Tokens
```

while

```text
Transformer Hidden States
```

are similar to:

```text
Audio Latents
```

This is not a perfect equivalence, but it builds the right intuition.

---

# Where Does SoundStream Fit?

At a high level:

```text
Audio
 ↓
SoundStream Encoder
 ↓
Audio Representation
 ↓
SoundStream Decoder
 ↓
Audio
```

The middle representation may be discussed as:

```text
Codec Tokens
```

or

```text
Audio Latents
```

depending on the implementation and level of abstraction.

The key point is:

> SoundStream learns its own representation instead of relying on a handcrafted representation such as a mel spectrogram.

---

# Connecting This Back to VoiceDM

Now let's revisit the VoiceDM pipeline.

Conceptually:

```text
Text
 ↓
VoiceDM Encoder
 ↓
DiT
 ↓
Audio Features
 ↓
SoundStream Decoder
 ↓
Audio
```

Notice what is happening.

VoiceDM is not trying to generate:

```text
Waveform Samples
```

directly.

Instead it generates a learned audio representation.

The SoundStream Decoder then converts that representation back into speech.

This is a much easier generation problem.

---

# A Very Important Mental Shift

When people first learn TTS, they often think:

```text
Text
 ↓
Audio
```

However, modern systems usually think:

```text
Text
 ↓
Audio Representation
 ↓
Audio
```

The representation in the middle is where most modern innovation happens.

Examples:

```text
Mel Spectrograms
Audio Latents
Codec Tokens
```

The entire architecture often revolves around choosing and generating the right representation.

---

# Mental Model

Keep the following picture in your head:

```text
Text World

Tokens
 ↓
Embeddings
 ↓
Hidden States
```

```text
Audio World

Codec Tokens
 ↓
Audio Latents
 ↓
Audio Decoder
```

The exact representation differs.

The fundamental idea is the same:

> Learn a compact representation that is easier for the model to generate than the final output itself.

---

# Key Takeaways

- Audio latents are learned compressed representations of audio.
- Codec tokens are discrete representations of audio.
- Neural codecs learn these representations directly from data.
- Generating latents or codec tokens is easier than generating waveforms directly.
- SoundStream and EnCodec are examples of codec-based architectures.
- Modern audio generation systems often operate in latent spaces.
- Audio latents play a role similar to hidden representations in language models.
- Codec tokens can be viewed as an audio vocabulary.

---

# Interview Takeaway

Modern audio systems rarely generate waveform samples directly. Instead, they often generate learned audio representations such as audio latents or codec tokens. These representations are produced by neural codecs like SoundStream and EnCodec. The decoder reconstructs the final waveform from this compressed representation, making audio generation significantly more efficient and scalable.
