# 02 - Frequency and Spectrograms: Why Transform a Waveform?

## Recap

In the previous lesson, we learned that audio is represented as a waveform.

A waveform is simply a sequence of sampled signal values:

```text
[0.12, 0.15, 0.08, -0.04, ...]
```

This representation contains all the information needed to reconstruct the original sound.

However, a waveform only tells us:

```text
How the signal changes over time
```

It does not directly reveal many of the patterns humans naturally perceive when listening to sound.

This leads to an important question:

> If the waveform already contains all the information, why do we transform it into another representation?

To answer that, we first need to understand the idea of frequency.

---

## What Is Frequency?

Think about a guitar string.

If the string vibrates slowly:

```text
Low Frequency
```

we hear a deeper sound.

If the string vibrates rapidly:

```text
High Frequency
```

we hear a sharper sound.

Frequency simply tells us:

> How fast a repeating pattern occurs.

Conceptually:

```text
Low Frequency

      /\
     /  \
    /    \
___/      \____



High Frequency

 /\ /\ /\ /\ /\ /\ /\ /
```

The second signal changes much faster, which means it has a higher frequency.

---

## Why Does Frequency Matter?

When humans listen to sound, we are not only listening to changes over time.

We are also listening to different frequencies.

Consider speech.

Different sounds contain different frequency patterns:

```text
Different speakers
Different vowels
Different consonants
Different emotions
```

Even if two people say the same word, their frequency content may be very different.

Humans naturally perceive these differences.

A raw waveform contains this information, but it is hidden inside thousands of signal values.

---

## Looking at a Waveform

Imagine someone says:

```text
"Hello"
```

The waveform might look like:

```text
Amplitude

      /\      /\        /\
     /  \    /  \      /  \
____/    \__/    \____/    \____

                         Time →
```

From this picture alone, it is difficult to answer questions like:

```text
Which frequencies are present?
Which frequencies are strong?
How do frequencies change over time?
```

Those details are buried inside the waveform.

---

## From Time Domain to Frequency Domain

A waveform represents sound in the:

```text
Time Domain
```

because its horizontal axis is time.

```text
Waveform

Amplitude
 ^
 |
 +-------------------> Time
```

Sometimes we want a different view.

Instead of asking:

```text
What is happening over time?
```

we ask:

```text
What frequencies are present?
```

This is called viewing the signal in the:

```text
Frequency Domain
```

Conceptually:

```text
Waveform
 ↓
Frequency Analysis
 ↓
Frequency Components
```

Instead of looking at individual samples, we look at the frequencies contributing to the sound.

---

## An Analogy with Images

Imagine looking at an image only as a long list of pixel values:

```text
[128, 120, 255, 90, ...]
```

All the information is technically present.

However, it is difficult for a human to understand.

Now imagine arranging those pixels into a 2D image.

Suddenly patterns become visible.

The same thing happens with audio.

A waveform contains all the information, but another representation may make important patterns easier to see.

---

## The Problem with Frequency Alone

Suppose we analyze an entire audio recording and determine:

```text
Frequency A exists
Frequency B exists
Frequency C exists
```

This still isn't enough.

Why?

Because speech changes continuously over time.

For example:

```text
"H"
 ↓
"E"
 ↓
"L"
 ↓
"L"
 ↓
"O"
```

The frequencies present at the beginning are not the same as the frequencies present later.

We need a representation that answers both questions:

```text
Which frequencies exist?
```

and

```text
When do they exist?
```

This leads us to one of the most important representations in audio:

```text
Spectrogram
```

---

## What Is a Spectrogram?

A spectrogram is a representation that shows:

```text
Frequency
       ^
       |
       |
       |
       +----------> Time
```

Each location tells us:

```text
How much energy exists
at a specific frequency
at a specific moment in time
```

Conceptually:

```text
             Time →

High Freq   █░░░█░░░░
Medium      ██░██░░░░
Low Freq    ███████░░

            ↑
        Frequency
```

You can think of it as an image of sound.

---

## Why Is a Spectrogram Useful?

A spectrogram exposes structures that are difficult to observe in a waveform.

Instead of seeing:

```text
0.12
0.15
0.08
-0.04
...
```

we can see:

```text
Which frequencies exist
How strong they are
How they change over time
```

Many speech patterns become much easier to represent in this form.

This is one reason why spectrograms became popular in speech and audio systems.

---

## Connecting This to Deep Learning

Think about computer vision.

CNNs receive:

```text
Height × Width
```

grids of pixels.

A spectrogram is also a 2D structure:

```text
Time × Frequency
```

Conceptually:

```text
Image

Height × Width
```

```text
Spectrogram

Frequency × Time
```

This is one reason early speech systems often used CNN-like architectures.

The input representation naturally resembles an image.

---

## Important Engineering Perspective

As model engineers, we should always ask:

```text
What representation enters the model?
```

Because different representations expose different information.

Examples:

```text
Raw Waveform
```

gives:

```text
Signal values over time
```

while:

```text
Spectrogram
```

gives:

```text
Frequency content over time
```

Neither is universally better.

They simply provide different views of the same signal.

The model architecture determines how that information is used.

---

## Mental Model

Keep the following progression in mind:

```text
Physical Sound
 ↓
Waveform
 ↓
Frequency Analysis
 ↓
Spectrogram
```

The waveform tells us:

```text
How the signal changes over time
```

The spectrogram tells us:

```text
What frequencies exist
and when they occur
```

This often makes speech patterns easier to represent and learn.

---

## Key Takeaways

- A waveform represents audio as signal values over time.
- Frequency describes how rapidly a pattern repeats.
- Different speech sounds contain different frequency patterns.
- A waveform contains frequency information, but it is not explicitly visible.
- A spectrogram shows how frequency content changes over time.
- Spectrograms are often described as images of sound.
- Many speech systems use spectrogram-based representations because important acoustic patterns become easier to observe.

---

## Interview Takeaway

A waveform contains all information about a sound signal, but frequency-related patterns are not explicitly visible. A spectrogram transforms the waveform into a time-frequency representation, showing which frequencies are present and how they change over time. This often provides a more informative representation for speech-related tasks and has historically been widely used in audio and speech models.
