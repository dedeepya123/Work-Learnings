# Thinking About Audio as Model Input

We already know that neural networks operate on tensors. So, when learning about audio models, the important question is not just what audio is, but what information the model receives and how that information is represented.

Think about an LLM. We start with text, convert it into tokens, and map those tokens into embeddings that the model can process. The model doesn't directly operate on the sentence as we see it.

Audio follows a similar idea, but its starting representation is different.

A digital audio recording can begin as a sequence of sampled signal values. Each value represents the amplitude of the sound signal at a particular instant in time.

For example, at a sampling rate of 16 kHz, we capture 16,000 samples per second. A three-second recording therefore contains 48,000 samples.

These samples are measurements of the sound signal, not individual words or phonemes. The model receives numerical values describing how the signal changes over time.

This gives us our first connection to model engineering: **the representation of the input affects what the model has to learn from it.**

If we give a model raw audio, it must learn useful speech patterns from the waveform samples. This is possible, but the samples themselves do not explicitly describe higher-level acoustic patterns.

One way to make those patterns easier to represent is to transform the waveform into another representation, such as a spectrogram or mel spectrogram. These representations describe aspects of how the signal's frequency content changes over time.

This is somewhat similar to how we choose representations in other areas of deep learning. In computer vision, an image is represented as pixel values arranged across height and width. In audio, we can represent the signal as samples over time or transform it into a representation that exposes its time-varying frequency content.

These representations are not interchangeable: each presents the signal differently, and the model architecture determines how it uses that information.

Understanding this distinction is useful when working on a voice model because we need to know what representation enters the model, what representation the model predicts or transforms, and how that representation eventually becomes audible speech.

That leads to the next concept: **why do we transform a waveform into a spectrogram instead of giving the model only the raw waveform?**

To understand that, we first need an intuition for what frequency tells us about sound and how a spectrogram captures changes in frequency over time.
